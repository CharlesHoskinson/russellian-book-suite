1. In the autumn of 2015 a Google researcher named Christian Szegedy fed an image of a panda into one of his own classifiers and was told, with 99.3 per cent confidence, that he was looking at a gibbon. The panda had not been altered in any way a human eye could detect. A faint pattern of noise, computed by another network and added pixel by pixel, had moved the image across the decision boundary while leaving the panda unchanged in every respect that mattered to a person. The classifier had not learned to see pandas. It had learned to find a region of high-dimensional space where panda-shaped pixels usually live.

2. The story has been told often enough that its edges have softened. It is repeated as a parable about adversarial examples, or about the brittleness of deep networks, or about safety. These are the right lessons if you are building classifiers. They are the wrong lessons if you are asking what the classifier knew.

3. What the classifier knew was a function from pixels to labels. The function was the result of an enormous fitting procedure over an enormous training set. The function was, by every measurable standard, extraordinarily good — better than any classifier before it, and on many benchmarks better than the median human annotator. The function was not, in any sense that survives Szegedy's perturbation, a representation of the animal.

4. This book is about that gap.

5. There is a particular kind of question philosophers have always reserved for themselves and which has, in the past decade, become an engineering problem. The question is whether a system that produces correct outputs across a vast range of inputs has therefore understood the domain of those inputs, or whether it has only recognised the surface patterns that correlate, in its training data, with the right answer. The question was easy to dismiss when the systems were small. It is no longer dismissible.

6. The question is older than the machines. Wittgenstein worried at it for forty years and produced a body of writing in which the verb "to know" splinters into a dozen contextual uses. Searle gave it the form of a man in a room shuffling Chinese cards. The cognitive scientists of the 1980s ran experiments on infants and on chimpanzees that turned the question inside out: maybe it is recognition all the way down, even in us. The philosophical literature is rich and not, on most weeks, much help to a working engineer.

7. The working engineer has a different problem. She has a system that scored 92 per cent on the held-out set, that ships on Monday, and that will be asked tomorrow to handle an input the training set did not anticipate. The held-out score does not tell her whether the system will recover. The training loss does not tell her. The architecture diagram does not tell her. She has, in effect, a system whose competence she cannot inspect.

8. The temptation, faced with that opacity, is to treat the system as a black box and trust the score. The temptation is reasonable. The cost of the temptation is that every black box eventually meets an input that the training set did not anticipate, and the cost of *that* meeting is borne by whoever was relying on the system at the moment of the meeting — the radiologist whose tumour-detection model misses the unusual case, the credit scorer whose model declines an applicant for reasons the applicant cannot challenge, the air-traffic controller whose conflict-detection alert fired on the wrong pair of aircraft.

9. So we have a question and we have a cost. What we do not yet have is a vocabulary that lets us tell the difference between a system that has understood and a system that has recognised, before the system meets its panda-gibbon moment in the field.

10. The book that follows is an attempt at that vocabulary. It is not a survey. It is an argument, and the argument is that the right distinctions can be found, and have begun to be found, in three places: in the mathematics of how representations factor, in the experimental design of how systems are probed, and in the older philosophical literature about what it would take for a representation to be *about* anything at all.

11. The argument owes a great deal to people who have spent careers on parts of it — Judea Pearl on the structure of causal inference, Yoshua Bengio on disentangled representations, Melanie Mitchell on abstraction, and many others whose work is cited in the bibliography. The argument also disagrees with some of them, in ways that will become specific as the chapters progress.

12. A book about understanding could be a long one. This one is short. The brevity is deliberate: the question of what machines understand is, at this moment in 2026, more urgent than it is finishable, and a short book that puts the right distinctions in the reader's hands is more useful than a long book that resolves them.

13. The chapters follow a single arc. The first two set up the question — what we mean by understanding, why pattern recognition is not it, why the difference matters for systems that will be deployed against high-stakes inputs. The middle three examine the three places where the difference can be detected: in the geometry of learned representations, in the response to systematic intervention, and in the structure of failure modes. The final two draw the consequences for how systems should be built, evaluated, and retired.

14. There is a way of reading the book that takes it as a defence of human cognition against machine cognition, a kind of last stand for understanding as a category that machines cannot enter. That is not the argument. The argument is that there is a distinction worth drawing, that the distinction is empirical rather than mystical, and that machines may eventually enter the side of the distinction that we currently reserve for ourselves — but that they are not there now, and pretending otherwise costs us our ability to tell.

15. Szegedy's panda did not become a gibbon when the noise was added. The classifier's relationship to the panda changed. What that relationship was, and was not, and how to know the difference — those are the questions this book is willing to take seriously, and the questions it asks the reader to take seriously too.
