1. The listicle is the most successful prose form of the last twenty years and the least useful. It has colonised the magazine, the blog, the academic abstract, the safety policy, the executive summary, and now the chapter draft. Wherever an argument is owed, a ranked enumeration is delivered instead, and the reader is invited to mistake the count for the case.

2. The defender of the form is easy to find. He is a content strategist, or a Substack columnist, or a product manager writing a one-pager, and he will tell you with the patience of someone who has been asked this before that ranked lists are how people read now. Attention spans. Mobile screens. The democratisation of expertise. He has a deck about this. He delivers the deck in the form of a list.

3. He is wrong, and the wrongness is not about reading. It is about thinking.

4. A ranked list, by its form, declares two things and conceals a third. It declares that there are N items worth knowing, and it declares an order that ranks them. It conceals the relations between the items — the dependencies, the causations, the trade-offs, the cases in which item three is incompatible with item six. The relations are the argument. The list is the surface on which the argument was meant to live, with the argument scraped off.

5. The defender, here, will object that the relations can be supplied by the reader. He will say that the list is a starting point, a scaffold, a "framework" — and that any reader who needs the relations can derive them. This is the move that does the listicle's real damage. It transfers the analytical labour from the writer, who was paid to perform it, to the reader, who was not.

6. The labour, transferred, is rarely performed. Most readers will not derive the relations. Most readers will skim the list, retain three items by recency or by colour, and act as though the items were independent. This is not the readers' failure. It is the form's design.

7. Consider one example. A safety policy that lists "seven principles for responsible deployment" is shaped exactly like the listicle. The principles are individually sensible. The relations between them — which take precedence, which exclude each other in edge cases, which are aspirational and which are auditable — are the substance of the policy. The principles can be put on a poster. The relations cannot. Guess which one survives the next reorg.

8. The list also distorts evidence. An argument that proceeds by paragraphs is forced to weight its claims; a strong claim earns more sentences, a weak one earns fewer, and the proportions show the reader where the argument is loaded. A list flattens the weights. Item one and item seven occupy the same visual footprint. The reader has no signal about which item the writer would actually defend under pressure.

9. The flattening serves the writer. A weak claim that would not survive a paragraph survives a bullet, because the bullet asks nothing of its surroundings. The bullet has no premises. The bullet has no consequences. The bullet has, in fact, no relation to the bullet above it or the bullet below — the form pretends each bullet is sovereign — and so the writer is freed from the obligation to make any claim cohere with any other.

10. The defender will say: but lists are clearer. The reader knows what is being asserted; the structure is plain. This confuses clarity with legibility. A list is legible — its boundaries are unambiguous — and clarity is something else. Clarity is when the reader knows not only what was claimed but why one claim follows from another and what would refute the chain. A list provides the first and refuses the second.

11. The listicle is most pernicious in the academic abstract. The abstract once carried the burden of the paper: it stated the question, the method, the result, and the consequence in a paragraph dense enough that an attentive reader could decide whether to read further. The current convention is four bullets — Background, Methods, Results, Conclusions — each containing a sentence that asserts without showing. The form has eaten the function.

12. It has eaten the function because the form is reviewed. A grant agency that funds a thousand abstracts has built tooling to score abstracts on the four-bullet template. The template selects for the bullets. The bullets select for the writers who write in bullets. The writers who write in bullets select for trainees who learn to write in bullets. In two generations the field has lost the skill of writing the dense paragraph that the abstract was meant to be.

13. There is a deeper objection that I have been keeping to the end. A list, by collapsing the relations between its items, also collapses the writer's accountability for those relations. The writer can argue that item three contradicts item six and be told that lists do not have to be internally consistent. The writer can be asked which item matters most and reply that the items are "equally important", a phrase that means nothing in any other prose form and yet passes unchallenged in this one.

14. The accountability is what we lose. A paragraph that argues for X is a paragraph that can be wrong about X; a list of seven principles, the third of which is X, is a list whose author can plausibly disown X if X turns out badly. The form has provided a built-in defence: any item can be retreated from without retreating from the list. The list survives the failure of any of its claims, because the list never made a claim that could fail.

15. The reader will recognise, by now, that this essay has been a paragraph form throughout — that no bullet has appeared, no ranked enumeration, no Background-Methods-Results — and that the argument has had to bear its weight in prose because the form forbade the alternative. The list was concealing the relations. The relations are what was at stake. The listicle is not the future of writing. The listicle is the future of writing that has stopped believing the relations matter.
