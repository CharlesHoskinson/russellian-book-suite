1. The claim ledger refuses to let a fact pass through it as a string. A claim arrives `proposed`, becomes `verified` when its locator text matches the source span, flips to `disputed` when antonym detection finds a contradiction, and ends its life as either `superseded` by a newer claim about the same triple or `refuted` by post-build QA. Five states. No others. The state field is an enum, not a memo.

2. The temptation, for an engineer building a fact store, is to leave status open. A free-form string accepts "tentative", "probably true", "marked for review", "deprecated, see ticket 4413", and a hundred others that accumulate as the team grows. Each variant is reasonable on the day it is added. The cost arrives later: no query against the ledger can be sure what it has retrieved.

3. The five-state machine forecloses that drift. The transition table is enforced by `claim_validator.VALID_TRANSITIONS`. A `proposed` claim may advance to `verified` or `disputed`; a `verified` claim may move to `disputed`, `superseded`, or `refuted`; a `disputed` claim may resolve back to `verified`; `superseded` and `refuted` are terminal. Any other write to the state field raises before it touches disk.

4. The invariant the five states preserve is simple to state and load-bearing in practice. Every claim in the ledger has a definite epistemic position — proposed, verified, disputed, superseded, or refuted — and the chain of transitions that brought it there is recoverable from the append-only event log. A query that asks for "verified claims about subject X" gets an unambiguous answer.

5. The invariant the five states make impossible is also simple: a claim cannot occupy two epistemic positions at once. There is no `verified-but-also-disputed`, no `superseded-by-tentative-claim`. The closest analogue is the `disputed` state itself, which exists precisely to hold the ambiguity when two verified sources contradict — and even that state has a single, terminal exit through verification or refutation.

6. The defender of the free-form field will say that real epistemic life is messier than five buckets. He is right when the question is philosophical, and wrong when the question is operational. A SHACL validator does not care about the fine grain of doubt; it cares whether the dataset conforms to the shape it is asked to check. Five buckets give SHACL a tractable shape. Twenty-seven do not.

7. Consider what the alternative costs at the boundary. When a chapter ships, `book-qa` runs the release gate. The gate's first check is "zero unsupported claims" — meaning zero claims whose state is anything other than `verified` or `disputed` with an attached counter-claim. A free-form status field forces every release gate to enumerate every variant that has ever been written. The enumeration goes stale. The gate fails silently.

8. The five states cost something too. They cost migration when a sixth state is genuinely needed: every consumer must update its expected-states set, every SHACL shape must extend its `sh:in` list, every query that asserts terminal-or-not must be revisited. The cost is paid up front and across the codebase, not amortised silently across the lifetime of the ledger. That is the trade.

9. The cost the five-state machine does not pay is the cost of analysis. Bayesian belief propagation, antonym-pair conflict detection, transitive-contradiction Datalog over the claim graph — each of these algorithms reads the state field as a categorical variable. A free-form string requires every algorithm to handle the long tail. An enum lets each algorithm dispatch on the five cases and move on.

10. There is a second invariant the state machine preserves that is easy to miss: the absence of `deleted`. The ledger is append-only. A claim that turns out to be wrong is not removed; it is transitioned to `refuted` or `superseded` and stays in the file forever. The state machine encodes the project's deeper commitment that the history of a claim is itself evidence and must not be edited.

11. Operators sometimes ask why `superseded` and `refuted` are distinct. They look similar from outside: both mark a claim that no longer holds. The distinction is in their cause. A `superseded` claim was correct against its source but has been overtaken by a more recent source that addresses the same triple. A `refuted` claim was wrong against its source and was caught by later QA. The first is the normal rhythm of knowledge accumulating; the second is a bug.

12. The bug case matters because it feeds backwards into the source-quality model. `proposed-transitions.jsonl` from `book-qa` records every refutation along with the source that caused the original claim. When refutation rates per source exceed a threshold, the source itself is downgraded — extractors weight it less in future runs. The two terminal states are not equivalent because their downstream consumers are not equivalent.

13. The five-state machine is not a complete model of how knowledge works. It is a model of how this pipeline writes, audits, and ships claims under a release gate. The model owes the reader an honest answer to "what does it know how to handle?" — and the five states are the honest answer. Anything that does not fit one of the five cases is something the pipeline cannot yet ship.

14. The pattern generalises. When a system needs to enforce a release gate over a heterogeneous body of work — claims, code changes, model outputs, expense reports — the first decision is what categorical states the work item can occupy, and the second is what transitions between them are legal. Skipping the first decision because "states are limiting" is the failure mode the five-state machine refuses.

15. Russell's observation that philosophy must "submit to the discipline of facts" applies here in compressed form. The discipline of a five-state machine is not that it captures every nuance of knowledge. The discipline is that it forces every claim in the ledger to declare what it is. A claim that will not declare itself is a claim the gate will not let through, and the gate is the only thing standing between the manuscript and the reader.
