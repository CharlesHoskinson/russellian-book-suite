# The printing press and the limits of disruption

In the workshop on the Mainz Hofgasse, around 1452, Johann Gutenberg pulled a sheet of damp paper from a wooden frame and inspected what was written upon it. The page carried two columns of the Latin Vulgate, set in metal type cast from a hand-cut mould. What he held was the prototype of an industrial process that would, within a decade, multiply book production by orders of magnitude across Europe. The man holding it did not know this. He died bankrupt, his press seized by his creditor Johann Fust, his Bibles sold under another man's name.

The technical innovation was not the press, which existed in oil and wine production, nor the ink, nor even movable type, which Korean and Chinese craftsmen had used for two centuries. It was the alloy. Gutenberg's lead-tin-antimony mixture cooled fast, cast clean, and yielded a letter that struck paper uniformly across thousands of impressions. It was a piece of metallurgy disguised as a literary revolution. The historians who have wished to find a single inventor of modernity have found one in Mainz, but the achievement is metallurgical and the metallurgist died poor.

What followed the invention was not what the inventor intended. Gutenberg had wanted to make Bibles cheaper. The press made indulgences cheaper too, and broadsheets, and tracts. Luther's ninety-five theses, posted at Wittenberg in 1517, were reprinted across Germany within weeks; without the press the Reformation would have been a local dispute with a friar. The Catholic Church, which had financed early presswork, found that the same instrument that copied its scripture also copied its opponents.

Pamphlets multiplied through the sixteenth century and the literate classes were drawn into argument they had not asked to join. The pamphleteer was a new figure: not a scholar but a tradesman with opinions, equipped to sell them by the dozen. Erasmus published in batches of three thousand. The official, the censor, the bishop, the magistrate — each found his pronouncements answered before he could circulate them. Authority adjusted its methods. It did not surrender.

The slowness of the actual change is what later accounts most often miss. Literacy in Europe in 1450 stood at perhaps ten per cent of adult men; in 1550, the figure was perhaps fifteen. The press had filled the libraries of universities and the studies of merchants, but the field labourer met it only through the catechism his priest read aloud. The disruption that has been described as instantaneous in the popular imagination was, on the ground, the work of three centuries.

The state, having understood the press more quickly than the press understood itself, brought it inside the licensing system. England's Star Chamber issued the Decree of 1586, requiring every printer to register with the Stationers' Company and to operate within the City of London. France placed similar restrictions. The press became, in effect, a regulated industry, and the new pamphleteer found himself in court as often as in trade. Innovation does not abolish gatekeepers; it produces a new generation of them.

A general truth lies under the German case. Technology enables; it does not cause. The press made the Reformation possible by lowering the cost of distributing argument, but the argument itself was Luther's, and the appetite for it was a German appetite. A Spanish Luther, equipped with the same press, would have been burned within a month. The machine sits inside a polity, and the polity decides what the machine is allowed to do.

The pattern repeats with the next disruptive press. The internet, in the early 1990s, was offered to its first users as a libertarian opening of the public discourse, free of gatekeepers, immune to capture. By 2010 the gatekeepers were a small handful of platforms, more concentrated than the Stationers' Company at its narrowest. The Decree of 1586 set out content that printers must not publish; the content moderation policies of the present-day platforms set out content that users must not post. Five centuries separate the two documents, and the family resemblance is closer than the rhetoric of either age would lead one to expect.

The word "disruption" carries an unearned compliment. It suggests that what is destroyed deserved to be destroyed and what replaced it deserves what it received. Neither is settled by the fact of replacement. The illuminated manuscript was destroyed as an economic product by the printed book, but the printed book did not, for at least a century, equal it in beauty, durability, or accuracy. Some kinds of destruction are sheer loss, even when the thing that destroyed them eventually equals or surpasses them. The replacement curve, in other words, has a trough; the trough is real; the trough has a cost; and the cost is paid by people who, having read the marketing of the disruptor, expected only gain.

A wooden frame, a lead alloy, a steady hand on the screw. The press was a machine, and like any machine it produced what its operator was paid to make. To assign it a will of its own — to call it the engine of modernity, the liberator of thought, the destroyer of the old order — is to imitate a habit the machine itself does not share, which is to attribute to one's tools the credit for one's choices. The press was indifferent. The men who used it were not, and the consequences belong to them.
