# The printing press and the limits of disruption

A man stood in a workshop on the Mainz Hofgasse in 1452 and lifted a damp page from under a wooden screw. The page held two columns of the Latin Vulgate, struck from metal type cast in a hand-cut mould — lead, tin, antimony, a recipe a goldsmith would recognise. The man was Johann Gutenberg. He was about to lose everything he had built. His creditor Johann Fust took the press, sold the Bibles under another man's name, and left Gutenberg to die in the Archbishop's almshouse.

What changed in the workshop was not the press, which already turned out olive oil and wine. What changed was the alloy. Gutenberg's lead-tin-antimony mixture cooled fast, struck clean, and held its shape across thousands of impressions — a piece of metallurgy disguised as a literary revolution. Two centuries earlier, Korean printers had cast moveable type in bronze; their bronze did not pour fine enough. The achievement most often called the beginning of modernity is the consequence of a casting trick. The metallurgist died poor.

What followed the invention was not what the inventor intended. Gutenberg wanted to make Bibles cheaper. The press made indulgences cheaper too, and broadsheets, and tracts, and the ninety-five theses Luther posted at Wittenberg in 1517, reprinted across Germany within weeks. Without the press the Reformation would have stayed a friar's local complaint. The Church had financed the early workshops. The same instrument that copied its scripture copied its opponents, and copied them faster than the scribes who had copied for the Church for a thousand years.

A new figure entered European life: the pamphleteer. Not a scholar, not a priest. A tradesman with opinions, equipped to sell them by the dozen. Erasmus published in batches of three thousand. The official, the censor, the bishop, the magistrate — each found his pronouncements answered before they reached the next town. Authority adjusted. It did not surrender. Authority is patient where the press is fast, and after the first shock the slower thing decides what the faster thing is allowed to print.

The slowness of the actual change is what most later accounts miss. Literacy in Europe in 1450 stood at perhaps ten per cent of adult men. In 1550 the figure stood at perhaps fifteen. In 1750, perhaps thirty. The press had filled the libraries of universities and the studies of merchants, but the field labourer in Pomerania met it only through the catechism his priest read aloud on Sunday. The disruption later imagined as a thunderclap was, on the ground, the patient work of three centuries — generations of children taught their letters in parish schools that did not yet exist when Gutenberg pulled his first page.

The state understood the press more quickly than the press understood itself. England's Star Chamber issued the Decree of 1586, requiring every printer to register with the Stationers' Company and to operate within the boundaries of the City of London. France placed similar restrictions through the Châtelet. Venice licensed at the press; Madrid licensed at the warehouse. Innovation does not abolish gatekeepers; it produces a new generation of them, and the new generation is rarely less restrictive than the one it replaces — only better at appearing to be.

A general truth lies under the German case. Technology enables; it does not cause. The press made the Reformation possible by lowering the cost of distributing argument; the argument was Luther's, and the appetite was a German appetite, shaped by a particular emperor, a particular pope, and a particular crisis of indulgences. A Spanish Luther, equipped with the same press, would have been burned within a month. The machine sits inside a polity, and the polity decides what the machine is allowed to do. The machine itself is indifferent.

The pattern repeats with the next disruptive press. The internet, in the early 1990s, was offered to its first users as a libertarian opening of the public discourse, free of gatekeepers, immune to capture. By 2010 the gatekeepers were a small handful of platforms — more concentrated than the Stationers' Company at its narrowest. The Decree of 1586 set out content that printers must not publish. The platforms' content-moderation rules set out content that users must not post. Five centuries separate the two documents, and the family resemblance is closer than the rhetoric of either age would lead one to expect.

The word "disruption" carries an unearned compliment. It suggests that what is destroyed deserved to be destroyed, and that what replaced it deserves what it received. Neither is settled by the fact of replacement. The illuminated manuscript was destroyed as an economic product by the printed book; the printed book did not, for at least a century, equal it in beauty, durability, or accuracy. The Plantin folios of the 1570s are competent. The Books of Hours from Bruges in the 1480s are not competent — they are extraordinary, and they are gone, and the replacement curve has a trough that the marketing of any disruptor never names.

A wooden frame, a lead alloy, a steady hand on the screw. That was the press. Like any machine it produced what its operator was paid to make, and the operator was paid by the Church, or the state, or the printer who hoped to sell ten thousand pamphlets in a season. To assign the machine a will of its own — to call it the engine of modernity, the liberator of thought, the destroyer of the old order — is to imitate a habit the machine does not share, which is to attribute to one's tools the credit for one's choices. The press was indifferent. The men who used it were not. The consequences belong to them.
