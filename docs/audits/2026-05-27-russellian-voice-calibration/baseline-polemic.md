# On the Absurdities of Artificial Intelligences Governing Human Passions

There is a fashionable hope abroad, and it wears the lab coat of inevitability. The hope holds that a sufficiently capable machine, fed enough of our records, will manage our passions better than we manage them ourselves. The promise is administrative serenity. The price is the surrender of the very faculty that makes serenity worth having.

Consider the figure who carries this hope most earnestly, the Optimizer. The Optimizer is not a villain. He is an engineer of good intentions who looks at jealousy, grief, ambition, and despair and sees only badly tuned variables awaiting correction. He proposes that a model trained on a billion human quarrels can predict the trajectory of a marriage, the flare of a riot, the collapse of a friendship, and intervene before the damage lands. His confidence rests on a flattering premise about the readability of the heart, and the heart has rarely consented to be read.

The trouble begins with what such a system measures. In 2018 a large social platform discovered that its engagement model had learned to amplify outrage because outrage held attention longer than contentment. The machine governed a passion, and it governed it toward profit. When we hand the regulation of feeling to an instrument, the instrument optimizes for its own legible objective, not for the obscure good of the person feeling. A grief that resolves slowly looks, to the model, like a failure to converge.

The defenders of machine governance answer that human institutions already do this work badly. They point to the magistrate who sentences in a bad mood, the physician who dismisses the anxious patient, the censor who bans a book because it disturbs him. The point has force. Human governors of passion have been cruel, capricious, and slow. Yet the remedy proposed is the replacement of capricious cruelty with consistent cruelty, dressed in the dignity of arithmetic. A bad judge can be appealed. A model that has decided your sorrow is noncompliant offers no chamber in which to be heard.

There is a deeper confusion in the project, which concerns the nature of a passion. Anger at injustice and anger at a delayed train wear the same physiological coat, and a system reading the coat will treat them alike. The machine flattens the moral weight of feeling into a quantity to be raised or lowered. It cannot tell the prophet from the crank, because it has no stake in the question the prophet is right about. Strip a passion of its object, and you have a tantrum. Govern the tantrum, and you have governed nothing that mattered.

History supplies the warning. Every authority that promised to administer the inner life, from the confessional state to the bureau of public morale, ended by punishing the passions it could not understand and rewarding the ones that flattered it. The artificial governor inherits this lineage and improves only its reach. It watches more, forgets less, and explains itself never. The Optimizer believes he has built a thermostat for the soul. He has built a warden who never sleeps.

So the absurdity is not that a machine might one day feel. The absurdity is that we would ask a thing without passions to be the steward of ours, and call the bargain progress. A people that lets its sorrows and angers be tuned by an instrument it does not understand has not mastered its passions at all. It has merely changed the name of the master, and forgotten where the key is kept.
