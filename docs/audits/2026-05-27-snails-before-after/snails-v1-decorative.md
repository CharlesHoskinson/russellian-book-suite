# On Snails

The snail enjoys a poor reputation, and bears it without complaint. We call a man a snail when we wish to insult his speed and his courage at once, and the snail, hearing nothing, continues across the wet stone about its business. There is a composure here that few of its critics have learned. The creature that cannot be hurried cannot be panicked, and it achieves this not by philosophy but by anatomy, which is cheaper and more reliable.

Consider first the matter of pace, since it is the only thing most people know about snails. The garden snail advances a few yards in an hour when the mood is on it, and rather less when the mood is off. A hare would find the comparison beneath notice, and a hare, in the one race posterity remembers, lost. The snail does not save time and does not try to. It has grasped, without being told, that time cannot be saved but only spent, and it spends its own without the anxiety that ruins ours.

The shell is where the snail keeps its mathematics. The curve it builds is the equiangular spiral, the same that James Bernoulli admired so greatly that he asked to have it carved upon his tomb. Bernoulli is in his tomb, and the curve with him; the snail, who never proved a theorem in its life, goes on drawing the figure faultlessly in lime. It is a humbling arrangement. The geometer earns the spiral by labour and dies; the mollusc secretes it and persists.

A snail carries its house, and this single fact disposes of a great deal of human striving. It pays no rent, signs no lease, and quarrels with no landlord over the deposit. When it tires of a place it removes itself and its entire estate together, at no cost beyond the journey. We have built whole industries upon the difficulty of doing what the snail does without a thought, and we have called the result civilisation.

The mouth of a snail is better furnished than its manners suggest. Along a ribbon called the radula it carries thousands of minute teeth, with which it rasps algae from stone and leaves from the gardener's lettuce. Set one against the glass of an aquarium at night and you can hear the work going on, a faint and patient filing. The snail is unarmed only to those who have not looked.

What the snail knows of the world it learns through a film of slime and a faint sense of smell. It is nearly blind; it has no ears at all; it reads its surroundings with two pairs of tentacles, the longer carrying eyes that retract at a touch, like a tradesman drawing in his sign. A creature so equipped ought, by every rule of epistemology, to be perpetually lost. It is not often wrong, however, about the location of the cabbage, which is more than several schools of philosophy can claim about anything.

The snail travels on a road of its own manufacture. It lays down a ribbon of mucus and glides upon it, so that it is at once the traveller, the vehicle, and the highway. A snail is said to cross the edge of a razor unharmed upon this carpet, and whether or not the experiment has been done kindly, the principle is sound. Men spend their fiercest passions disputing the ownership of ground they did not make. The snail makes its ground and leaves it behind without regret.

In the matter of love the snail has arranged things with a strangeness that would embarrass a moralist. It is both sexes at once, and settles nothing on that account, for two snails must still find each other. Before they mate, certain kinds fire a small dart of lime into the partner, a courtship conducted, against all metaphor, with a genuine arrow. Cupid, it turns out, was a naturalist before he was a poet. The snail has merely declined to pretend the business is gentler than it is.

A snail grows by adding to its shell, and the shell forgets nothing. Each season leaves its line, so that the house is also a diary, written in calcium and legible to anyone patient enough to read a thing so small. The animal spends much of its life gathering lime against the cost of building, an economy of the most prudent kind. It does not borrow the material of its safety. It earns it slowly and keeps the accounts in its own back.

The snail has solved the problem of waiting, which the philosophers since Zeno have managed only to describe. When the world turns hot or cold or dry, it withdraws into the shell, seals the door with a parchment of dried slime, and suspends itself until conditions improve. It can hold this posture for months, and in extremity for years. We have invented a hundred narcotics against tedium; the snail simply stops, and resumes when stopping is no longer required.

Rome thought well enough of the snail to farm it. There were enclosures, the cochlearia, where the creatures were fattened on bran and boiled wine until they grew too heavy for their houses, and Pliny records the names of the gentlemen who improved the practice. The same animal has thus been a delicacy in one country and a plague in the next, escargot in a Burgundian kitchen and vermin in an English row of lettuces. The snail is indifferent to the distinction, which lives entirely in the eye and the appetite of the man holding the fork.

To the gardener the snail is not a philosopher but an enemy, and the war between them is older and more even than the gardener likes to admit. He sets out saucers of beer and rings of copper and goes to bed satisfied; he wakes to find the hostas reduced to lace. There is something instructive in a contest where the side with the larger brain, the opposable thumb, and the chemical industry at its back can manage, in a good year, a draw. Slowness, well defended, is a kind of strength.

The snail has been made to stand for sloth, which is counted among the deadly sins, and in other ages for prudence and persistence, which are praised. The trait did not change; the men judging it stood in different degrees of a hurry. A pace that looks like laziness to a soldier looks like wisdom to a sage, and the snail, supplying neither opinion, lends itself to both. Most of our moral certainties, looked at closely, have a snail at the bottom of them: something quite neutral that we have dressed in praise or blame to suit ourselves.

The slowest of creatures is, by a quiet irony, among the best travelled. Darwin, who let nothing small escape him, found that snails would survive a long soaking and ride across water on the feet of ducks, settling islands they could never have reached on their own account. The snail conquers continents without intending a single step of the campaign. It is carried to its empire asleep, which is the only dignified way to acquire one.

Whether a snail suffers, no one can honestly say, and the honest course is to be slow about denying it. The creature draws back from harm, prefers some things to others, and behaves throughout as though its small life mattered to it. This proves little, but it forbids the easy conclusion that there is nobody at home. A decent regard for minds unlike our own begins with the admission that we have never been inside one, not even a man's, and certainly not a snail's.

The snail keeps no theology and holds no opinion for which it would kill a neighbour. It has never burned a heretic, signed a manifesto, or gone to war over the true shape of the shell. A creature incapable of dogma is incapable of most of the crimes that fill our histories. I do not offer the snail as a model of every virtue, the lettuce standing as witness against it; but in the one matter of not being certain past the evidence, it improves upon the larger part of mankind.

The economy of the snail is the despair of economists, since no fee will attach to it. It asks little, wastes less, and leaves the half of the leaf it does not need standing for the next comer. It hoards nothing it cannot carry and covets nothing it cannot eat. No system of accounts has found a way to call this poverty, though every system has found a way to call our opposite habit wealth.

For all its armour the snail is not safe, armour seldom being sufficient. The song thrush has learned to carry the shell to a chosen stone, an anvil it returns to season after season, and to break the house upon it for the tenant within. The snail's defence is its dwelling and its dwelling is its grave, the two being, as so often, the same address. Nature is sparing with most things and spends its ironies freely.

The plainest lesson of the snail is its quarrel with our worship of speed. We have arranged our lives so that everything may be done faster and nothing, on that account, any better. The snail does each thing slowly and once, and is spared the peculiar modern labour of doing things quickly and then again. It will never invent the wheel, and it will never have to outrun the consequences of having invented it.

We began by pitying the snail and may end, if we are candid, by envying it a little. It carries its home on its back, owes no man anything, hurries toward no goal it will be sorry to reach, and meets its end without having spent its life in dread of it. It asks of the universe only to be left to its lettuce; and the universe, which refuses far larger requests every day, now and then consents.
