# On Snails

The dislike most people feel for the snail is not a dislike of the snail at all, but of slowness, and slowness has been libelled. We measure accomplishment by distance covered in a given time, which is the right measure for messengers and racehorses and for almost nothing else that matters. A proof in mathematics is not better for being reached quickly. A friendship is not improved by haste. The snail, which does one thing and then the next, follows a method with more to recommend it than its reputation allows.

The shell of the snail grows on a precise mathematical curve, the logarithmic spiral, and this has been offered, by people who should know better, as evidence of a designing mind in nature. It is evidence of nothing of the kind. The snail knows the curve no better than a planet knows the ellipse it travels; the regularity lies in our description of the motion, not in the creature's intention. What the shell shows is the less comfortable truth that intricate order can arise with no mind to intend it, and that the argument from design has always mistaken our pleasure in a pattern for proof of a patterner.

A snail carries its house on its back and is at home wherever it happens to be. This is usually offered as a curiosity, but it deserves a moment's envy. The creature owns what it can carry and wants what it can reach, and in the snail these two quantities are the same. We have built a civilisation on the difference between them.

Whether a snail can suffer is a question we cannot answer, and the honest response to a question we cannot answer is to leave it open. The snail draws back from salt and from fire; it prefers the damp to the dry and the dark to the glare; it behaves throughout as though its condition mattered to it. None of this proves an inner life. None of it licenses the confident denial of one either, and the confident denial is the commoner error.

The garden snail is male and female at once, an arrangement that has given no trouble to the snail and a good deal to those who wish nature to endorse their own. Nature endorses nothing. It keeps what survives, and the snail's solution has survived a very long time. The lesson is not about snails but about the standing folly of asking the natural world to settle questions that belong to ethics.

Almost everything a snail knows of the world reaches it through chemistry and touch. It is nearly blind, entirely deaf, and carries its eyes on stalks that it draws in at the first alarm. One would expect such a creature to be helpless, and it is not. It finds its food, evades its enemies, and manages its affairs on a ration of information that would reduce a metaphysician to despair. Knowledge sufficient for life is a far smaller thing than knowledge sufficient for certainty, and most creatures, men included, get along quite well on the first.

The modern world worships speed and has confused it with progress. We do everything faster than our grandfathers did and are not visibly wiser or kinder for the saving. I do not suggest that we move at the snail's pace. I suggest only that speed is not a virtue in itself, which is a thing the snail illustrates and the railway timetable denies.

For most of its history the snail has been an emblem of sloth, which the Church reckoned among the deadly sins; in other ages it has stood for patience and persistence, which are praised. The animal did not change between the verdicts. What changed was the temper of the people who passed them. A great many of our firmest moral judgments are of just this kind: a neutral fact with a label pinned to it by whoever was in a hurry.

Darwin, who overlooked nothing small, found that snails would survive a long soaking and ride across water on the feet of wading birds, reaching islands they could never have crossed by their own effort. The snail colonises places it never meant to reach, by means it knows nothing of. The general truth is worth more than the instance: in nature as in history, the outcome of an action rarely resembles the intention behind it, and the creatures that prosper are not, as a rule, the ones with the grandest designs.

When the weather turns against it, the snail stops. It seals the mouth of its shell with a film of dried slime and suspends its life until the conditions it needs return, and it can hold that posture for a season or, at need, for years. We have invented religion, philosophy, and a whole pharmacopoeia of distractions to help us through the intervals in which nothing useful can be done. The snail gets through them by not insisting that something be done.

The snail holds no opinions, and this, which sounds like a poverty, repays a closer look. It has never burned another snail for heresy, gone to war over the right reading of a leaf, or died for a conviction it had no way to test. The capacity for belief, which we count among our glories, is also the engine of most of the cruelty in our history, and a creature that lacks it is spared both. I would not trade the human mind for the snail's, the lettuce-beds standing witness against it. But in the one matter of holding no belief beyond the reach of evidence, the snail keeps a discipline the wisest of us reach only by labour and lose at the first provocation.

The shell that is the snail's defence is also, in the end, its undoing. The song thrush has learned to carry it to a chosen stone and break it there, returning to the same anvil season after season, so that a little midden of shards collects beside a favoured rock. The armour that keeps out the drought does not keep out the bird. Most armour is like that: it answers one danger and says nothing about the next.

It is one thing to speak of "the snail" and another to watch a particular snail cross a particular wet flagstone at dusk, its horns testing the air, the silver track lengthening behind it, the whole deliberate business conducted without an audience and without hurry. The abstraction gives you a definition; the creature gives you something to attend to. Most of what is worth knowing is learned in the second way.

The snail takes what it needs and stores nothing it cannot carry, and an economist inspecting it would call it a failure, since it makes no surplus and enters into no trade. Whether that verdict falls on the snail or on the economics I leave to the reader. I note only that we have built an elaborate apparatus for turning the inability to be satisfied into a sign of health, and that the snail has contrived to live without one.

Between the gardener and the snail there runs an old war, and it is more even than the gardener likes to admit. He has the larger brain, the opposable thumb, and the chemical industry at his back; the snail has appetite, numbers, and the night. By morning the hostas are lace. There is something to be learned from any contest in which intelligence, so heavily armed, can manage no better than a draw.

Haste is the enemy of thought as much as of digestion. The man who must have his conclusion at once will seize the first that offers and defend it with the energy he ought to have spent in doubting it. The snail reaches its leaf in time and eats it with attention; I have known arguments conducted with less.

Certainty is a state the snail never enters and the human mind enters far too easily. We are built to feel sure, and the feeling is no evidence of being right; the heat of a conviction measures the temperament of its holder and not the truth of the thing held. The snail, having no convictions, is never betrayed by them. This is not wisdom in the snail, which has none. It is a tolerable picture of what a part of wisdom would look like in us.

I have no wish to recruit the snail as a sage. It is a small, wet, short-lived animal that eats the garden and is eaten by the thrush, and it understands nothing of the uses I have put it to; the uses are mine. A thing need not be wise to be instructive. It needs only to be looked at carefully by someone willing to learn.

What, then, does the snail offer the patient observer? Not a doctrine, for it has none, and not an example to be copied, for we are not snails. It offers a correction: that slowness, sufficiency, and the want of dogma are not the failings we take them for, and that a careful look at a small life returns more than a hasty look at a large one.

That is a great deal to ask of a creature with no opinions and no hurry. It is also about as much as our weightier subjects have ever given us, and they have charged a good deal more for it.
