# The Foundations of Mathematical Certainty

Certainty in mathematics has always been borrowed rather than owned. The Greeks supposed they had paid for it outright, and Euclid drew up the receipt around 300 B.C. in the form of his axioms. Yet an axiom is a loan one declines to question, not a debt one has discharged. For two thousand years the geometers admired the elegance of the bill and forgot that no one had ever audited it. The audit, when it came, found the parallel postulate written in disappearing ink.

Euclid's fifth postulate is the first place where the edifice betrays its own foundations. It asserts what no eye can verify and no compass can draw: that two lines, produced far enough, will meet. The ancients felt the awkwardness and tried for centuries to demote the postulate to a theorem. Saccheri, in 1733, assumed its falsehood and labored to reach absurdity, then mistook his own discomfort for a contradiction. He had in fact discovered a new geometry and walked away from it in horror.

Non-Euclidean geometry settled an old question by refusing to answer it. Lobachevsky and Bolyai, working apart in the 1820s, denied the fifth postulate and found that nothing broke. Gauss had reached the same result earlier and suppressed it, fearing the outcry of those he called the Boeotians. What their work showed was modest and devastating at once. Geometry does not tell us the truth about space; it tells us the consequences of assumptions we are free to vary.

Space, then, ceased to be the guarantor of mathematical truth. So long as one geometry described the world, certainty could lean on the world for support. Once three geometries stood as equals, the support was withdrawn, and each system had to stand on its own internal honesty. This was a demotion dressed as a discovery. Mathematics learned that it had never been describing the heavens, only describing what would follow if the heavens were thus and so.

The calculus offered power long before it offered any account of itself. Newton and Leibniz, in the 1670s, built an engine that predicted planets and bridges with indecent accuracy. They built it on the infinitesimal, a quantity that was somehow both nothing and not nothing, depending on the line of the argument. Bishop Berkeley named these the ghosts of departed quantities, and the insult was deserved. The mathematicians kept the engine running and declined to inspect the fuel.

Berkeley's mockery was the sound of a foundation cracking under applause. He observed that the practitioners discarded a quantity as zero in one step and divided by it in the next. No accountant would tolerate such bookkeeping, yet the results were correct to as many decimals as one cared to compute. Here lay the scandal of the eighteenth century. A method that could not justify a single one of its operations nonetheless never erred.

Cauchy began the long work of paying for what had been taken on credit. In his lectures of the 1820s he set out to define the derivative without recourse to the infinitely small. He spoke of limits, of quantities approaching others as near as one pleased, and the ghosts began to thin. His definitions still leaned on a verb, "approaches," that hid a motion no one had analyzed. He had cleaned the house and left one locked room.

The locked room held the word "approaches," and Weierstrass opened it. Around 1860 he replaced the language of motion with the language of inequalities, the epsilon and the delta that every student now endures. There is no approaching in his account, no quantity in flight, only the flat assertion that for every challenge a response exists. The price of this rigor was the abolition of intuition. He had purchased certainty by surrendering the picture, and the bargain has never been refused since.

Arithmetic was assumed to be the bedrock, and that assumption was the next to go. The whole numbers seemed beyond suspicion; even the geometers, doubting their lines, did not think to doubt their counting. Yet the real numbers, on which the calculus stood, had never been defined at all. Mathematicians used the continuum daily and could not have said what a single irrational number was. The bedrock turned out to be a borrowed word.

Dedekind supplied the missing definition by an act of audacious modesty. In 1872 he proposed that an irrational number simply is a cut in the rationals, a partition of every fraction into those below and those above. The square root of two had been a length one despaired of writing down; he made it the boundary of two infinite sets. Nothing could be more artificial, and nothing has worn better. He had defined the continuum without once appealing to a line one could see.

Cantor went further and counted the infinite, which polite mathematics had forbidden. Beginning in the 1870s he asked whether one endless collection could be larger than another, a question the ancients had dismissed as ill-formed. He proved that the integers and the fractions are the same size, then proved that the points on a line are not. The infinite, it emerged, came in grades. A man had brought a measuring rod to a country every authority had declared unmeasurable.

The diagonal argument is the most economical thunderbolt in the subject. Cantor supposed the real numbers could be listed, then constructed a number differing from the first in its first digit, the second in its second, and so on forever. No list could contain it, because it was built to disagree with every entry. Four lines of writing overturned the conviction that infinity admits of no degrees. Rarely has so much been undone by so little.

Frege saw that arithmetic itself wanted a foundation, and resolved to supply one out of logic alone. In the Begriffsschrift of 1879 he invented a notation able to express the inference no ordinary language could pin down. His ambition was to show that the truths of number were not facts about the world, nor intuitions of the mind, but consequences of logic that no rational being could deny. He worked nearly twenty years at it. The aim was the grandest in the history of the subject, and it failed by a single sentence.

Peano, meanwhile, reduced arithmetic to a handful of starting points and a strict symbolism. By 1889 he had shown that the whole of number theory could be spun from three undefined ideas and five axioms. The economy was startling; a science that touched everything rested on almost nothing. His symbols banished the little hedging phrases of ordinary proof and left only what followed from what. He had built the spine of arithmetic and left the skull to Frege.

Frege's system was complete, in his own eyes, as the second volume went to press in 1903. He had derived number from class and class from logic, and the long campaign appeared won. Then a letter arrived from a younger man in England, and it contained a single question about the class of all classes that are not members of themselves. The question could be neither answered yes nor answered no. Frege added a stricken appendix admitting that the foundation had given way beneath the building.

The paradox of that letter was mine, and I take no pride in it. The class of all classes not members of themselves both must and must not contain itself, and the contradiction is immediate. It was no isolated curiosity but one of a brood; Burali-Forti had already found trouble in the largest ordinal, and others soon turned up in the largest cardinal and in the liar's ancient sentence. Each shared a family likeness, for a totality permitted to refer to itself produced an object that destroyed the totality. The disease was not in this class or that one. It lay in the freedom with which the subject had been allowed to form its collections.

Whitehead and I spent ten years building a fence around the contradiction. The Principia Mathematica, finished in 1910, ranked propositions and classes in a hierarchy of types, so that no collection might speak of itself. The cure worked, in the sense that the known paradoxes could no longer be stated. It worked the way amputation works. We had saved arithmetic by mutilating the logic from which we hoped to draw it.

Several hundred pages of Principia pass before the reader learns that one and one are two. The proposition is proved at last, with the dry remark that it is occasionally useful. I record this not as a boast but as a confession of the cost. To establish what every child knows, we had to traverse machinery that almost no adult could follow. Certainty, when finally cornered, proved to live a great distance from common sense.

Hilbert proposed to make the matter of certainty itself a subject of mathematics. Around 1900, and more sharply in the 1920s, he asked for a proof that the axioms of arithmetic could never lead to a contradiction. The proof was to use only the safest, most finite reasoning, so that no one could object that it begged the question. It was a magnificent and reasonable demand. He wanted certainty about certainty, and he very nearly persuaded the age that he would get it.

The Hilbert program asked for three guarantees, and each seemed within reach. Mathematics should be complete, so that every truth admits a proof; consistent, so that no falsehood does; and decidable, so that a procedure could settle any question in finite time. Stated together they describe a paradise of the mechanical. Every problem would have its answer, and the answer could be ground out by rule. The dream lasted until a young Austrian read it carefully.

Gödel dismantled the program in 1931 with a proof of terrible elegance. He showed how a system rich enough for arithmetic could talk about its own statements, and then built a sentence that asserted its own unprovability. If the system could prove it, the system was inconsistent; if it could not, the sentence was a truth beyond the system's reach. Either way completeness was impossible. The instrument Hilbert had asked for was shown not to be lacking but to be forbidden.

The first incompleteness theorem leaves every honest formal system with truths it cannot reach. There will always be a proposition, plainly true to us who stand outside, that the system can neither prove nor refute. One may add it as a new axiom, and a fresh unprovable truth springs up at once. The horizon recedes exactly as fast as one advances toward it. Arithmetic is inexhaustible, which is another way of saying it can never be wholly secured.

The second theorem is the crueler of the two. Gödel showed that no consistent system adequate for arithmetic can prove its own consistency. The one assurance Hilbert most wanted, that the machinery would never grind out a falsehood, is precisely the assurance no machinery can give about itself. A system strong enough to be interesting is too weak to vouch for itself. We are left trusting our tools for reasons the tools cannot supply.

Certainty in mathematics survived these defeats, but it changed its address. It no longer lives in the axioms, which we now choose rather than read off from heaven. It lives in the inference, in the cold fact that given the premises the conclusion cannot be evaded. This is a narrower estate than the ancients imagined they owned. We have lost the truth of our starting points and kept only the rigor of our steps.

The hypothetical character of mathematics is its lasting discovery, not its embarrassment. Pure mathematics asserts that if certain things hold, then certain others must; it does not assert that the first things hold at all. Whether any axiom is true of the actual world is a question for the physicist, and not an easy one. The mathematician is free of that burden and pays for the freedom in scope. He knows that his conclusions follow, and he does not pretend to know that they are true.

Applied mathematics smuggles back the very claims that pure mathematics renounced. When the engineer uses the calculus to throw a bridge across a river, he assumes that space is Euclidean and that his numbers measure real beams. The assumption is excellent and might be false; the bridge stands until it does not. Here certainty of inference meets uncertainty of premise, and the two are forever distinct. The bridge tests the premise, never the logic.

Intuition, once the proud parent of geometry, has been retired to an advisory post. Time and again the obvious has proved false: that a continuous curve must somewhere have a tangent, that a whole exceeds its parts, that the line and the plane contain different quantities of points. Each of these convictions felt like bedrock, and each gave way. Weierstrass exhibited a curve continuous everywhere and smooth nowhere, and intuition has never recovered its authority. We keep it now for inspiration and forbid it the courtroom.

The history of foundations is the history of demands for proof outrunning supplies of proof. Each generation discovers that what its fathers took for granted requires demonstration, and sets about the demonstration, and uncovers a new thing taken for granted beneath it. Euclid trusted space; Newton trusted the infinitesimal; Frege trusted the class; Hilbert trusted that consistency could be proved within. The regress does not terminate in bedrock. It terminates in our decision to stop digging.

What we have, in the end, is not certainty but its disciplined imitation. We cannot prove our axioms, and we cannot prove that our axioms will not betray us, and we proceed regardless. The proceeding is not faith, for faith does not check its steps; it is the narrowest possible trust, extended only to the validity of inference. That much we may have, and that much has built the largest reliable structure the human mind possesses. It is less than Pythagoras hoped for and more than he had any right to expect.

The lesson of two thousand years is that mathematics gives knowledge of implication and nothing besides. We never learn that any proposition is true; we learn only that one proposition entails another. The ancients confused these and bequeathed us the dream of necessary truth about the world. The dream was beautiful and the waking has been profitable. We traded an idol of certainty for a working instrument, and the instrument, unlike the idol, has never once let us down where its terms were honored.
