# On Snails

Watch a snail cross a wet flagstone at dusk and the question changes. It is not what is wrong with the creature; it is what is wrong with us, who decided long ago that haste was a virtue, and the snail, who decided long before us that it was not. The longer tentacles probe the air, the shorter feel the ground; the body draws itself forward by waves of muscle along the underside of the foot. Six minutes pass and the flagstone is half-crossed. The crossing will be finished without an audience and without hurry.

Begin with the pace, since it is the only thing most people know. The garden snail, *Cornu aspersum*, advances a few yards in an hour when conditions favour it, and less when they do not. A hare would find the comparison beneath notice; a hare, in the one race posterity remembers, lost. The snail does not save time and does not try to. Time, as the snail seems to know without being told, is not a substance that can be saved at all.

It is sometimes said that the snail's shell, with its precise logarithmic curve, is evidence of a designing intelligence in nature. The case is harder to make than it looks. The shell does indeed grow as an equiangular spiral; Jacob Bernoulli loved that curve so intensely that he asked for it on his tomb at the Basel Münster, with the inscription *eadem mutata resurgo* — though changed, I rise the same. The stonemason carved an Archimedean spiral instead. The mathematician who had spent his life on the logarithmic case did not get one on his stone, and the curve he had earned by labour was lost in the one place he had hoped to find it. The snail goes on secreting the right curve in lime, generation after generation, with neither knowledge of what it is doing nor anyone to mistake the design.

A snail carries its house wherever it goes. The shell is hollow on a calcium spiral, secreted from a mantle gland just inside the lip and added to as the animal grows; the foot extends below; the breathing pore opens and closes on the right side. The whole arrangement weighs less than a postage stamp and is sufficient to its purpose. There is no rent.

The mouth of a snail is better furnished than its manners suggest. Along a ribbon called the radula it carries thousands of minute teeth, made of chitin and arranged in rows that wear and are replaced. With this ribbon it rasps algae from stone and leaves from the gardener's lettuce. Place a snail on the glass wall of an aquarium at night, hold an ear close, and you will hear the work going on — a faint and patient filing. The snail is unarmed only to those who have not looked. The teeth are the smallest serious teeth in any garden, and they have been at work since the late Cambrian.

What the snail knows of the world it learns through chemistry and touch. It is nearly blind. It is entirely deaf. It carries its eyes on the longer pair of tentacles, eyes that retract at a touch, like a tradesman drawing in his sign at the first sign of weather. A creature so equipped ought, by every rule of epistemology, to be perpetually lost. It is not often wrong, however, about the location of the cabbage.

The snail travels on a road of its own manufacture. It lays down a ribbon of mucus and glides upon it, so that it is at once the traveller, the vehicle, and the highway — three roles in one body. A snail is said to cross the edge of a razor unharmed on this carpet, and whether or not the experiment has been done kindly, the principle is sound. The mucus contains hygroscopic glycoproteins; the foot moves on waves of muscular contraction that biologists call pedal locomotion; the trail evaporates within an hour, leaving the road undone behind the traveller.

In the matter of love the snail has arranged things with a strangeness that would embarrass a moralist. It is both sexes at once, and settles nothing on that account, for two snails must still find each other. Before they mate, certain species fire a small dart of calcium carbonate into the partner — the brown garden snail (*Cornu aspersum*), the Roman snail (*Helix pomatia*) — a courtship conducted, against all metaphor, with a genuine arrow. Cupid, it turns out, was a naturalist before he was a poet.

A snail grows by adding to its shell, and the shell forgets nothing. Each season leaves its line, so that the house is also a diary, written in calcium and legible to anyone patient enough to read a thing so small. The animal spends much of its life gathering lime against the cost of building. The shell tells you its age and its lean seasons and its good ones, but it does not tell you what the snail thinks of any of them.

The snail has solved the problem of waiting, which the philosophers since Zeno have managed only to describe. When the world turns hot or cold or dry, it withdraws into the shell, seals the door with a parchment of dried slime called the epiphragm, and suspends itself until conditions improve. It can hold this posture for months. In extremity, for years. A specimen of the desert snail *Helix desertorum*, collected dry and glued to a card in the British Museum in 1846, revived four years later when a curator thought to soak it.

Rome thought well enough of the snail to farm it. The agricultural writer Varro records in *Rerum Rusticarum* (III.xiv.4) that one Fulvius Lippinus was the first to keep a *cochlearium* — an enclosure of stones and turf where the creatures were fattened on bran and boiled wine until they grew too heavy for their houses. Pliny later took up the story; Apicius gave a recipe. The same animal has been a delicacy in one country and a plague in the next, *escargot* in a Burgundian kitchen and vermin in an English row of lettuces, and the distinction lives entirely in the eye of the man holding the fork.

To the gardener the snail is not a philosopher but an enemy, and the war between them is older and more even than the gardener likes to admit. He sets out saucers of beer, rings of copper, sharp grit at the base of the hosta, slug pellets of iron phosphate, blue plastic pellets of metaldehyde. He goes to bed satisfied. He wakes to find the hostas reduced to lace. There is something instructive in a contest where the side with the larger brain, the opposable thumb, and the chemical industry at its back can manage, in a good year, a draw.

In medieval psalters the snail appears in the margin facing a knight in retreat, an emblem of the deadly sin of sloth. The Victorians, for reasons of their own, made it a figure of patience and persistence instead. The pace did not change between the verdicts — only the temper of the people passing them. What looks at first like a story about the snail turns out, on inspection, to be a story about the moods of the people drawing the marginalia, and the snail has nothing to say about its career as a symbol either way.

The slowest of creatures is, by a quiet irony, among the best travelled. Darwin, who let nothing small escape him, found that snails would survive a long soaking and ride across water on the feet of wading ducks, settling islands they could never have reached on their own account. The snail conquers continents without intending a single step of the campaign. It is carried to its empire asleep, which is the only dignified way to acquire one.

Whether a snail suffers, no one can honestly say, and the honest course is to be slow about denying it. The creature draws back from salt. The creature draws back from fire. It prefers the damp to the dry and the dark to the glare; it behaves throughout as though its small life mattered to it. None of this proves an inner life. None of it licenses the confident denial of one either, and the confident denial is the commoner error.

The snail keeps no theology and holds no opinion for which it would kill a neighbour. It has never burned a heretic, signed a manifesto, gone to war over the true shape of the shell, or formed a committee to investigate the orthodoxy of another snail. Whether this is virtue or merely the absence of the capacity for vice is a question the snail does not have the equipment to answer; the lettuce-beds, which it does eat, stand witness against the simpler reading.

It is tempting to make the snail an emblem of an economy without waste: it asks little, hoards nothing it cannot carry, and leaves the half of the leaf it does not need for the next comer. The temptation should be resisted. The snail does these things because its body is small and its needs are correspondingly small; because it cannot manufacture surplus; because the muscle to drag a hoard is muscle it cannot spare. Asceticism is not the right word for an organism that has no other option. The snail teaches us nothing about restraint that biology does not already make compulsory.

For all its armour the snail is not safe, armour seldom being sufficient. The song thrush, *Turdus philomelos*, has learned to carry the shell to a chosen stone, an anvil it returns to season after season, and to break the house on it for the tenant within. A little midden of shards collects beside the favoured rock. The snail's defence is its dwelling, and its dwelling is its grave, the two being, as so often, the same address.

The plainest lesson of the snail is its quarrel with our worship of speed. We have arranged our lives so that everything may be done faster and nothing, on that account, any better. The bullet train crosses Honshu at three hundred kilometres an hour. The fibre-optic cable carries a packet across the Atlantic in seventy milliseconds. The drone delivers the parcel in twenty minutes. The snail does each of its things slowly and once, and is spared the peculiar modern labour of doing them quickly and then again.

A note, finally, on the essayist. I notice that I have spent the last several pages enlisting the snail in a series of objections to the modern world, and the snail, which I admit I have never asked, may take no view of the modern world at all. It is a small, wet, short-lived animal that eats the leaves of the garden and is eaten by the thrush, and it understands nothing of the uses I have put it to; the uses, and the satisfaction in them, are mine. A reader who has been impressed by my snail has been impressed mainly by me, which is a poor exchange for the price of an essay. The snail, having no opinion about itself either, would not recognise the figure it has been made to cut here, and would prefer, on inspection, the wet flagstone.
