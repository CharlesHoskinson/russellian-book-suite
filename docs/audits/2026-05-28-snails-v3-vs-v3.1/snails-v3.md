# On Snails

Watch a snail cross a wet flagstone at dusk and the question changes. It is not what is wrong with the creature; it is what is wrong with us, who decided long ago that haste was a virtue, and the snail, who decided long before us that it was not. The longer tentacles probe the air, the shorter feel the ground; the body draws itself forward by waves of muscle along the underside of the foot. Six minutes pass and the flagstone is half-crossed. The crossing will be finished without an audience and without hurry, and the snail will have spent its evening doing exactly what it set out to do — which is more than most of us can say of an evening.

Begin with the pace, since it is the only thing most people know. The garden snail, *Cornu aspersum*, advances a few yards in an hour when conditions favour it, and less when they do not. A hare would find the comparison beneath notice; a hare, in the one race posterity remembers, lost. The snail does not save time and does not try to. It has grasped, without being told, what most philosophers have failed to teach: that time cannot be saved but only spent, and the snail spends its own without the anxiety that ruins ours.

The shell is where the snail keeps its mathematics. The curve it builds is the equiangular spiral, the same that James Bernoulli wished carved on his tomb. Bernoulli is in his tomb. The curve is with him. The snail, which never proved a theorem in its life, goes on drawing the figure faultlessly in lime, generation after generation, with neither pride nor record. The geometer earns the spiral by labour and dies. The mollusc secretes it and persists. There is a moral here, but I leave the snail to draw it.

A snail carries its house, and this single fact disposes of a great deal of human striving. It signs no lease, quarrels with no landlord, pays no rent. When it tires of a place it removes itself and its estate together at no cost beyond the journey. The mortgage industry, the moving company, the legal profession that arbitrates between them — each could be folded up tomorrow without inconvenience to the snail. We have built whole industries on the difficulty of doing what the snail does without a thought, and we have called the result civilisation.

The mouth of a snail is better furnished than its manners suggest. Along a ribbon called the radula it carries thousands of minute teeth, made of chitin and arranged in rows that wear and are replaced. With this ribbon it rasps algae from stone and leaves from the gardener's lettuce. Place a snail on the glass wall of an aquarium at night, hold an ear close, and you will hear the work going on — a faint and patient filing. The snail is unarmed only to those who have not looked. The teeth are the smallest serious teeth in any garden, and they have been at work since the late Cambrian, which is more than the gardener can say.

What the snail knows of the world it learns through chemistry and touch. It is nearly blind. It is entirely deaf. It carries its eyes on the longer pair of tentacles, eyes that retract at a touch, like a tradesman drawing in his sign at the first sign of weather. A creature so equipped ought, by every rule of epistemology, to be perpetually lost. It is not often wrong, however, about the location of the cabbage. Several schools of philosophy have made smaller claims about larger questions with less reliability. Knowledge sufficient for life is a far smaller thing than knowledge sufficient for certainty, and most creatures, ourselves included, get along quite well on the first.

The snail travels on a road of its own manufacture. It lays down a ribbon of mucus and glides upon it, so that it is at once the traveller, the vehicle, and the highway — three roles in one body. A snail is said to cross the edge of a razor unharmed on this carpet, and whether or not the experiment has been done kindly, the principle is sound. Men spend their fiercest passions disputing the ownership of ground they did not make. The snail makes its ground and leaves it behind without regret.

In the matter of love the snail has arranged things with a strangeness that would embarrass a moralist. It is both sexes at once, and settles nothing on that account, for two snails must still find each other. Before they mate, certain species fire a small dart of calcium carbonate into the partner — the brown garden snail (*Cornu aspersum*), the Roman snail (*Helix pomatia*) — a courtship conducted, against all metaphor, with a genuine arrow. Cupid, it turns out, was a naturalist before he was a poet. The snail has merely declined to pretend the business is gentler than it is.

A snail grows by adding to its shell, and the shell forgets nothing. Each season leaves its line, so that the house is also a diary, written in calcium and legible to anyone patient enough to read a thing so small. The animal spends much of its life gathering lime against the cost of building. It does not borrow the material of its safety. It earns it slowly, and it keeps the accounts in its own back. There is no clearer demonstration of a virtue often praised and rarely practised, which is to pay for what you take, at the moment you take it.

The snail has solved the problem of waiting, which the philosophers since Zeno have managed only to describe. When the world turns hot or cold or dry, it withdraws into the shell, seals the door with a parchment of dried slime called the epiphragm, and suspends itself until conditions improve. It can hold this posture for months. In extremity, for years. A specimen of the desert snail *Helix desertorum*, collected dry and glued to a card in the British Museum in 1846, revived four years later when a curator thought to soak it. We have invented a hundred narcotics against tedium. The snail simply stops, and resumes when stopping is no longer required.

Rome thought well enough of the snail to farm it. There were enclosures, the *cochlearia*, where the creatures were fattened on bran and boiled wine until they grew too heavy for their houses. Pliny the Elder records the names of the gentlemen who improved the practice — Fulvius Hirpinus among them, of the second century BC. Apicius gave a recipe. The same animal has thus been a delicacy in one country and a plague in the next, *escargot* in a Burgundian kitchen and vermin in an English row of lettuces. The snail is indifferent to the distinction. The distinction lives entirely in the eye and the appetite of the man holding the fork.

To the gardener the snail is not a philosopher but an enemy, and the war between them is older and more even than the gardener likes to admit. He sets out saucers of beer, rings of copper, sharp grit at the base of the hosta, slug pellets of iron phosphate, blue plastic pellets of metaldehyde. He goes to bed satisfied. He wakes to find the hostas reduced to lace. There is something instructive in a contest where the side with the larger brain, the opposable thumb, and the chemical industry at its back can manage, in a good year, a draw. Slowness, well defended, is a kind of strength.

The snail has stood, in different ages, for different sins and virtues. The medieval Church counted it among the figures of sloth and put it in the margins of psalters facing a knight in retreat. The Victorians, who had different anxieties, made it a figure of patience and persistence. The pace did not change. The men judging the pace stood in different degrees of a hurry. Most of our moral certainties, looked at closely, have a snail at the bottom of them — a neutral fact dressed in praise or blame to suit the temperament of the dresser.

The slowest of creatures is, by a quiet irony, among the best travelled. Darwin, who let nothing small escape him, found that snails would survive a long soaking and ride across water on the feet of wading ducks, settling islands they could never have reached on their own account. The snail conquers continents without intending a single step of the campaign. It is carried to its empire asleep, which is the only dignified way to acquire one. Empire-builders who marched would do well to study the method.

Whether a snail suffers, no one can honestly say, and the honest course is to be slow about denying it. The creature draws back from salt. The creature draws back from fire. It prefers the damp to the dry and the dark to the glare; it behaves throughout as though its small life mattered to it. None of this proves an inner life. None of it licenses the confident denial of one either, and the confident denial is the commoner error. A decent regard for minds unlike our own begins with the admission that we have never been inside one — not even a man's, and certainly not a snail's.

The snail keeps no theology and holds no opinion for which it would kill a neighbour. It has never burned a heretic, signed a manifesto, gone to war over the true shape of the shell, or formed a committee to investigate the orthodoxy of another snail. A creature incapable of dogma is incapable of most of the crimes that fill our histories. I do not offer the snail as a model of every virtue — the lettuce-beds stand witness against it — but in the one matter of not holding a belief past the reach of evidence, it keeps a discipline the wisest of us reach only by labour and lose at the first provocation.

The economy of the snail is the despair of economists, since no fee will attach to it. It asks little, wastes less, and leaves the half of the leaf it does not need standing for the next comer. It hoards nothing it cannot carry. It covets nothing it cannot eat. No system of accounts has found a way to call this poverty, though every system has found a way to call our opposite habit wealth. Karl Polanyi, studying the embedding of markets in older social arrangements, would have recognised in the snail the original disembedded eater — outside the price system entirely, and none the worse for it.

For all its armour the snail is not safe, armour seldom being sufficient. The song thrush, *Turdus philomelos*, has learned to carry the shell to a chosen stone, an anvil it returns to season after season, and to break the house on it for the tenant within. A little midden of shards collects beside the favoured rock. The snail's defence is its dwelling, and its dwelling is its grave, the two being, as so often, the same address. Nature is sparing with most things and spends its ironies freely.

The plainest lesson of the snail is its quarrel with our worship of speed. We have arranged our lives so that everything may be done faster and nothing, on that account, any better. The bullet train crosses Honshu at three hundred kilometres an hour. The fibre-optic cable carries a packet across the Atlantic in seventy milliseconds. The drone delivers the parcel in twenty minutes. The snail does each of its things slowly and once, and is spared the peculiar modern labour of doing them quickly and then again. It will never invent the wheel, and it will never have to outrun the consequences of having invented it.

We began by pitying the snail and may end, if we are candid, by envying it a little. It carries its home on its back. It owes no man anything. It hurries toward no goal it will be sorry to reach. It meets its end without having spent its life in dread of it. It asks of the universe only to be left to its lettuce — and the universe, which refuses far larger requests every day, now and then consents.
