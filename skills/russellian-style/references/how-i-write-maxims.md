# Russell's Sentence-Craft Maxims

Distilled from Bertrand Russell's 1956 essay "How I Write." These are mechanical rules for sentence construction. Apply them after the structural pass, before final review.

## Maxim 1 — Lexical economy
Never use a long expression or word if a short one will suffice, provided exact technical fidelity is maintained.

## Maxim 2 — Partition qualifications
If a statement requires multiple qualifications, do not bury them in dependent clauses. Partition them into separate, distinct sentences.

## Maxim 3 — Linear trajectory
The beginning of a sentence must never lead the reader to an expectation that is contradicted or fundamentally altered by its end.

## Maxim 4 — One movement per sentence
A sentence carries one logical movement. A second movement is a second sentence.

## Maxim 5 — Verbs over nominalizations
"The script provisions the server" beats "the provisioning of the server is performed by the script."

## Maxim 6 — Names over pronouns when ambiguity is possible
Pronouns whose antecedents could be misread are replaced by the explicit name.

## Maxim 7 — Cut the modifier budget
For every adjective and adverb, ask: does this sentence remain true and complete without it? If yes, cut.

## Worked example — Russell's 62→25 word translation

**Before (62 words, academic sociology bloat):**

> Human beings are completely exempt from undesirable behaviour-patterns only when certain prerequisites, not satisfied except in a small percentage of actual cases, have, through some fortuitous concourse of favourable circumstances, whether congenital or environmental, intersected at the appropriate developmental stage of the individual organism in such a manner as to render the resulting personality compatible with the maintenance of socially constructive habits.

**After (25 words, axiomatic):**

> All men are scoundrels, or at any rate almost all. Those men who are not must have had unusual luck, both in their birth and in their upbringing.

The 60% reduction is not stylistic; it is the difference between a claim that can be evaluated and one that cannot.
